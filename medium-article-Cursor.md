# How a Spiral-Wound Reverse Osmosis Membrane Module Separates Water

A kitchen reverse osmosis system does not push every drop of feed water through a finer version of a coffee filter. Pressure drives some water across a dense membrane. The water that crosses is called permeate, or product. The water that stays behind is called concentrate, or brine, and it leaves by a different outlet with the material the membrane did not pass.

That split is the whole machine. If a diagram shows clean water and lets the rejected stream fade out, it is hiding the part that costs water and still needs a destination.

This piece follows a 34-second schematic film of a spiral-wound reverse osmosis membrane module. The film is a teaching picture. It is not a certified design, not a manufacturer drawing, and not a rating for any product you can buy. The process description below is grounded in U.S. EPA WaterSense material on point-of-use reverse osmosis. The spiral-wound plate in the film is a labeled schematic of a common element form. EPA does not publish that plate as a construction drawing.

## Start with the train, not the cartridge

People say "the RO filter" and point at one cylinder. The cylinder is the membrane module. The treatment train around it is what makes the module usable, and what makes the water balance visible.

On the way in, feed water usually meets pretreatment. A particulate stage can hold back sand, rust, and other solids that would scrape or clog the membrane surface. That stage really is a size separation. It is not the reverse osmosis step. A second housing is often there to condition the water and protect the membrane. Some oxidants damage common thin-film membranes, so many systems include a carbon stage. Many is not all. The film labels that housing as conditioning and does not name the media.

Then comes pressure. Some point-of-use systems run on household line pressure. Others add a booster pump. EPA's WaterSense specification covers both. Neither fact justifies printing a single pressure setting on a general diagram. The driving force is pressure applied to the feed. The number depends on the water, the membrane, the recovery the designer chose, and whether a storage tank is pushing back. A honest picture shows energy going in and refuses the setpoint.

After the module, two pipes leave. Product water may go to a faucet, often by way of a storage tank. Concentrate goes somewhere else. In a typical under-sink system, EPA describes that stream as going to the drain, where it becomes wastewater. A reuse kit is a different design. It is not the default, and it is not what happens if you simply stop drawing the line.

## What "spiral-wound" is asking you to picture

The working element in most residential and commercial reverse osmosis modules is a spiral-wound cartridge inside a pressure vessel. The film opens that idea with a section window cut into the housing. The vessel stays one piece. The window is a drawing convention, the same kind of cut a mechanical drawing uses. It is not an instruction to unbolt the element while it is running, and it is not an exploded view pretending the hardware flies apart.

Inside, the schematic shows a pack wound around a central collection tube. A membrane leaf is a sandwich: membrane sheet, a permeate collection layer, membrane sheet again. A feed spacer sits between leaves and keeps a channel open so water can move along the surface. Feed travels roughly parallel to the tube, from one end of the vessel toward the other. Permeate travels inside the leaf, inward, toward holes in the tube, and then along the tube to the product end. Concentrate stays in the feed channel and leaves at the far end.

To make those two directions readable, the film unrolls one leaf onto an enlarged plate. The caption says the leaf does not unwind in operation. Thickness is exaggerated. Several leaves are indicated. The count, the length, and the materials are not specified, because they are not universal and because inventing them would turn a schematic into a fake datasheet.

If you remember one geometric fact, remember the directions. Feed and concentrate move along the module. Permeate moves across the leaf and into the tube. They are not the same pipe drawn twice.

## The selective layer is not a colander

This is the sentence that usually goes wrong in popular science.

A dense reverse osmosis membrane is not a screen with one hole size that lets water molecules through and bumps every dissolved ion off the rim. Larger particles can be stopped upstream, in pretreatment, by something much closer to a size cut. Dissolved material at the membrane is a different problem. Water crosses a continuous selective layer. Salts, metals, and other dissolved species are rejected to different degrees, or pass to different degrees, depending on the substance, the membrane, and the operating conditions.

The film draws that layer as a solid band, with a porous support underneath. The support is there so the thin selective layer has something to sit on. The support is not the selective step. Texture in the support is not a row of pores you are supposed to count. Marks for dissolved material are rings, diamonds, and squares so they do not all look like the same ball bearing. They travel with the feed. They pack closer together toward the downstream end, which is the picture for a remaining stream that has become more concentrated. They do not bounce off a drawn grate.

Two of those marks are allowed to end with the product. That is deliberate. Product water in this explanation is not sterile, not pure, and not empty. EPA's public description says point-of-use reverse osmosis systems can potentially reduce contaminants such as lead, arsenic, certain volatile organic compounds, PFAS, and, in some cases, bacteria and viruses. "Can potentially" is doing real work in that sentence. Under the NSF/ANSI 58 standard that WaterSense requires for labeled systems, every certified system has to meet a baseline reduction for total dissolved solids, at least 75 percent in that standard's terms. Claims for specific contaminants are tested when a manufacturer makes them. A baseline on total dissolved solids is not a promise about every species, and it is not a sterility test. The film refuses the word sterile except to say the picture is not that claim.

Slow motion and enlarged scale are labeled because the crossing you see is not a physical clock and not a measured thickness. Time compression is labeled on the wide shots because a real tank fill is not five seconds long.

## Two exits, and a balance with no percentage on it

Follow one water path and one solute path and the machine gets simple.

Some water crosses the selective layer, moves through the collection layer to the central tube, and leaves as product. Feed water that does not cross continues to the concentrate outlet. Both streams are still water. The difference is what they carry, and how much of the feed each one represents.

EPA has published the uncomfortable version of that split for point-of-use systems. A typical unit can send five gallons or more of reject water down the drain for every gallon of treated water. Some inefficient units waste even more, on the order of ten gallons of reject for one gallon of product. WaterSense labeled systems, under the specification current as of late 2024, have to do better: 2.3 gallons or less to the drain per gallon produced, which is an efficiency rating of at least 30 percent. Those figures describe a product class and a labeling rule. They are not the recovery of the module drawn in the film. The film's balance graphic is widths and words. The words say the widths are explanatory, not a recovery rating. Putting 30 percent on that drawing would steal a certification number and glue it to a cartoon.

There is a second reason to keep the number off the picture. Efficiency and recovery are not casual synonyms once a storage tank is in the story. A tank is convenient. It also pushes back on the membrane as it fills, and that back pressure changes how much water you actually get at the faucet. EPA's WaterSense write-up spends real space on that distinction, and on automatic shutoff, which stops reject flow when the system is not treating. The film does not simulate tank back pressure. It says so by refusing a rated recovery, not by inventing a calm 50/50 split.

What the film does conserve is the set of dissolved marks. Twelve go in. Ten finish in the concentrate collector. Two finish with the product. Looping ticks in the pipes show direction only. They are not a counted volume. If you are checking a diagram, count the rejects. If they vanish, the diagram is lying about the mass balance even when the prose sounds responsible.

## Concentrate is a managed stream

The last frame is supposed to feel finished, not magical. The pump still shows an energy input. Small violet marks sit on the feed, the product, and the concentrate. They mean "a real train would be watched here." They are not a named instrument, not a setpoint, and not an alarm. Product liquid is visibly present, and it is labeled as not a sterile claim. Concentrate liquid is visibly present, with the rejected marks in it, and the label is a destination: to a drain, or other permitted handling.

That destination matters more than the membrane poetry. Reject water is not a special fluid that ceases to exist at the property line. In the ordinary point-of-use layout EPA describes, it becomes wastewater. Some buildings can reuse concentrate for a non-drinking purpose. That is a plumbing and water-quality decision, not a feature you get by ignoring the pipe. The film keeps the collector on screen through the held ending so the stream cannot be cropped out by the last camera move.

Energy gets the same treatment. Pumping is a cost, even when the pump is small, and even when a system has no electric booster and is spending line pressure instead. The gold arc on the pump is actuation. It is not a wattage and not an efficiency badge.

## What to ask before you treat this as a buying guide

Reverse osmosis is a strong tool and a thirsty one. EPA has been explicit that the WaterSense label is not a campaign to make people buy RO instead of a treatment method that wastes no water. If a carbon filter or another certified process solves the contaminant you actually have, RO may be the wrong machine. If RO is the right machine, the useful questions are narrow:

- Is the whole system certified to NSF/ANSI 58, not just described as "RO" on a box?
- Which contaminants does that certification cover, and which ones are only a marketing sentence?
- What efficiency rating was measured, including a storage tank and any automatic flush, rather than a recovery number from an open bench test?
- Where does concentrate go in this building, and who maintains the membrane and the pretreatment?
- Does the system shut off reject flow when the tank is full?

None of those answers are in the schematic. A schematic that pretends to include them is the dangerous kind.

The useful schematic does a smaller job. It shows two exits, a dense layer instead of a magic grate, a pretreatment step that is not the membrane, and a concentrate stream that is still in the room when the animation ends. That is enough to stop the worst misunderstanding. It is not enough to size a skid, pick a membrane, or clear a glass of water.

## Questions people actually type

**Does reverse osmosis remove everything in the water?**
No. Rejection depends on the contaminant, the membrane, and the conditions. Certified systems have to meet specific reduction tests for the claims they make. A general diagram should not show product water as empty.

**Is RO water sterile?**
Do not treat it as sterile because it passed through a membrane. Microbial performance is a separate claim, and storage tanks can become their own problem if they are neglected. EPA's consumer guidance talks about draining and sanitizing tanks for that reason. This film does not show those maintenance steps, and it says the product is not a sterile claim.

**What pressure should the module run at?**
There is no single setting that belongs on a general explainer. Line pressure, booster pumps, temperature, salinity, and tank back pressure all move the result. Ask for the tested range on a real product sheet, not for a number from an animation.

**Where does the dirty water go?**
It is concentrate, not a puddle the membrane ate. In a typical point-of-use installation it goes to the drain and becomes wastewater. Any reuse path has to be designed and permitted. It should stay on the drawing.

**What is a spiral-wound element?**
A cartridge of membrane leaves and spacers wound around a perforated tube, inside a pressure vessel. Feed flows along the leaves. Permeate spirals inward to the tube. Concentrate leaves the other end of the feed channel. The unrolled leaf you see in a teaching drawing is a schematic, not a picture of the element opening itself.

## A last distinction

Filtration, in the ordinary sense, stops something because it does not fit. Reverse osmosis, in the sense that matters for dissolved material, is a pressure-driven separation across a dense selective layer, with a leftover stream that gets saltier as it goes. The module is the wound element that gives that separation somewhere to happen. The train is the pretreatment, the pressure, the two outlets, and the decision about where concentrate is allowed to go.

Keep those pieces in the same frame. The moment any one of them disappears, the explanation has started to advertise.
